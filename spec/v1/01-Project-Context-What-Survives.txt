Measuring Legislative Alteration of Voter-Approved Ballot Initiatives — Project Context and Design

0. How to read this document
This document is the complete context for a research and software project. It is written to be read cold, by someone — or something —
that has not been part of the conversation that produced it. It covers what ballot initiatives are, what problem this project addresses, why
the project took the shape it did, what will be built, and how the output will be defended.

A companion document, Prototype Specification, describes the first buildable increment. That document assumes this one has been read.




1. Summary
In twenty-one U.S. states, citizens can enact statutes directly by ballot initiative, bypassing the legislature entirely. In many of those
states, the legislature can then amend or repeal what voters passed.

Sometimes that amendment implements the measure. Sometimes it hollows it out. At present, both events are recorded identically — as
“the legislature altered this initiative” — because no dataset measures how much of a voter-enacted law survives.

This project builds that measurement, and publishes it as a browsable, citable resource. For each altered initiative, every provision
voters enacted is tracked through the legislature’s amendments and coded for what happened to it, what kind of provision it was,
whether the change expanded or narrowed the measure, and by what drafting mechanism.

The one-sentence framing: existing methods measure whether the text changed; this measures whether the law still does what
voters voted for.




2. Background: what a ballot initiative is

2.1 The basic mechanism
A ballot initiative is a law drafted and proposed by citizens rather than by the legislature, then enacted by direct popular vote. It
originated with Progressive Era reformers who believed state legislatures had been captured by railroad and corporate interests, and
designed the initiative as a bypass.

Twenty-one states permit initiated statutes. The process, in outline:

1. Draft. Write the statutory text that will become law.
2. File and review. Submit to the secretary of state or attorney general, who reviews for form and drafts the official ballot title and
   summary voters will see.
3. Gather signatures. Collect a threshold number of registered voters’ signatures, usually a percentage of the last gubernatorial vote,
   often with geographic distribution requirements.
4. Verify. Election officials validate signatures. Many campaigns fail here.
5. Litigate. Opponents frequently sue pre-election over single-subject rules, ballot title wording, or signature validity.
6. Vote. Simple majority in most states.


2.2 Who actually files initiatives
Legally, any citizen may. Practically, almost none do alone. Filers are typically coalitions — unions, advocacy organizations, industry
groups, or a single wealthy backer. Missouri’s Proposition B (2010) was filed by a coalition including the Humane Society of the United
States and the ASPCA. Drafting requires lawyers; qualification requires money and usually paid signature circulators.

The “ordinary citizen” framing is part of the initiative’s founding mythology. Whether what voters enacted really belongs to the voters is
a live question, and it cuts in both directions — a point this project should surface rather than assume.


2.3 Direct versus indirect
This distinction describes only the path to the ballot.

   Direct initiative — sufficient signatures put the measure directly on the ballot. The legislature is not involved.
   Indirect initiative — sufficient signatures send the measure to the legislature first. The legislature may enact it as written (in which
   case there is no popular vote), or decline, in which case it proceeds to the ballot.
Critical clarification: direct and indirect describe what happens before enactment. Post-enactment legislative alteration applies to
both. A direct initiative is not immune. Missouri’s Proposition A was a direct initiative approved by voters, and the legislature repealed
most of it anyway.

The indirect route does create one distinctive maneuver. In Michigan in 2018, two indirect initiatives qualified — a minimum wage
increase and a paid sick leave requirement. The legislature adopted both before the election, which removed them from the ballot, then
amended both in the December lame-duck session. Michigan requires a three-quarters supermajority to amend a law voters approved at
the polls; because these never reached the polls, a simple majority sufficed. The Michigan Supreme Court ruled against the maneuver in
2024. This “adopt and amend” pattern is coded as its own flag in the schema.


2.4 Statute versus constitutional amendment
    Initiated statute — creates or changes ordinary law. The legislature can later amend or repeal it, subject to state rules.
    Initiated constitutional amendment — changes the state constitution. The legislature cannot unilaterally alter it; only voters can.

There is no mechanism for citizens to amend the U.S. Constitution by initiative. “Constitutional amendment” in this context always
means a state constitution.

State constitutions are far easier to amend than the federal one and correspondingly longer, containing detailed policy provisions — tax
rates, gambling rules, program funding — that would never appear federally. This is exactly what makes the constitutional route
attractive to campaigns: not that the policy is constitutional in character, but that constitutional text is beyond legislative reach.

Scope consequence: this project covers initiated statutes only, because those are the measures capable of being legislatively altered.
This excludes several of the most frequently cited examples in this area, including Florida’s Amendment 4 (felon re-enfranchisement) and
Florida’s medical marijuana measure, both of which are constitutional amendments. The exclusion is stated explicitly on the project’s
Method page.


2.5 The three rule regimes
States differ in how much protection a voter-enacted statute receives. This variation is the project’s central doctrinal variable.

3. The phenomenon

3.1 Scale
Among initiated statutes approved from 2010 through 2018, 28 of 99 were repealed or amended as of April 2019. Across 2010–2024,
Ballotpedia counts roughly 30 of about 150 initiated statutes altered, with key provisions repealed in five. Marijuana was the most-
altered topic, followed by elections. Maine altered four of eight approved measures; Colorado and Oregon three of five each.


3.2 Four worked examples
Missouri Proposition A (2024) — severe, fast, selective. Approved by nearly 58% of voters in November 2024. It raised the
minimum wage to $15, indexed future adjustments to the Consumer Price Index beginning 2027, and required most private employers to
provide earned paid sick time at one hour per thirty worked. The sick leave requirement took effect May 1, 2025. On July 10, 2025, HB
567 was signed, repealing the paid sick time provisions entirely effective August 28 — roughly four months after they began — and
repealing the inflation indexing. The $15 wage survived.

The pattern this illustrates: the legislature kept the visible headline number and removed both an entire entitlement and the escalation
mechanism that would have mattered most over twenty years.

Missouri Proposition B (2010) — partial. Approved 51.6%. Required large-scale dog breeders to provide adequate food, water,
housing, space, veterinary care, exercise, and rest between breeding cycles; capped breeders at fifty breeding dogs; created a
misdemeanor offense. Repeal legislation was filed within a month. SB 113, passed April 2011 (Senate 20–14, House 85–71), eliminated
the fifty-dog cap, the breeding frequency limit, and the cage-height requirement. The general care standards and the criminal penalty
survived.

Why paired with Proposition A: same state, same unrestricted regime, fourteen years apart, different topics, and two different survival
profiles. If both are simply coded “altered,” that difference is invisible.
South Dakota Initiated Measure 22 (2016) — total, immediate, referendum-proof. A campaign finance, lobbying, and ethics
package creating a publicly funded campaign finance program and a state ethics commission. Approved November 2016. HB 1069 was
introduced January 20, 2017 and signed February 2, 2017 — roughly two months after passage. Because the bill carried an emergency
clause, citizens were barred from mounting a veto referendum to overturn the repeal.

Utah Proposition 4 (2018) — slow, litigated, reversed. An anti-gerrymandering measure. SB 200 (2020) rescinded its core reforms.
In 2024 the Utah Supreme Court held that the legislature had overstepped, grounding the limit in the state constitutional right to alter
or reform government. The legislature responded within weeks by placing a counter-amendment on the ballot.


3.3 The escalation cycle
Alteration provokes response. After HB 567 repealed Proposition A’s sick leave provisions, Missouri advocates began pursuing the same
policy as a constitutional amendment — the same substantive goal in a vehicle beyond legislative reach. Arizona’s purpose-fidelity rule
was itself enacted by citizen initiative.

This suggests a second-order consequence worth measuring: legislative alteration may be pushing ordinary policy into state
constitutions, where it is hard to fix when badly drafted. The schema records prior and subsequent similar measures so this cycle is
traceable.




4. How the project arrived here
The project passed through four abandoned directions. This history is recorded because the reasons for abandonment constitute the
design criteria that the current project must satisfy.

Model-bill diffusion in state AI legislation. Text-reuse analysis of the state AI legislative wave. Abandoned after a January 2026
ACLU/Brown report covered adjacent ground, and after a decision to move away from AI policy as subject matter.

Ballot title fidelity. Measuring the gap between official ballot summaries and the statutes they describe. Methodologically sound and
genuinely novel — existing work measures readability, not accuracy — but tabled. The provision-decomposition machinery developed for
it carried forward.

Post-Grants Pass municipal ordinance drafting. Testing whether camping ordinances enacted after the 2024 Supreme Court
decision matched the premises the Court asserted. The faculty advisor objected on two grounds: a null result (ordinances remain facially
neutral) was predictable in advance, because cities had been drafting neutral ordinances for decades and the decision gave them no new
reason to stop; and text is a weak proxy for what happens to people. Both objections were correct.

Doctrinal migration after Grants Pass. Mapping where litigation went once the Eighth Amendment route closed. Better, but pivoted
away from.

Design criteria that survived this history:

1. The finding must not be predictable from the armchair. A hypothesis with no mechanism produces an uninformative null.
2. Any null result must still be a finding.
3. Textual analysis must connect to consequences, not stop at text.
4. Novelty claims must be checked against existing literature before commitment, and stated narrowly.

The shift to a visualization tool. After the measurement project was presented, the faculty advisor redirected toward a browsable
resource rather than a paper — a tool scholars could use, structured around five analytical frames: what exactly changed, where changes
concentrate, whether an amendment expands or narrows, how quickly changes occurred, and how the change was described. The
current design is that tool.




5. Existing research, and the gap

5.1 What exists
Political science. The canonical work is Gerber, Lupia, McCubbins and Kiewiet, Stealing the Initiative, which found California
legislators displacing initiative content and subverting implementation of voter-ratified measures. A later study of Maine used elite
interviews to push back, finding legislators there reluctant to meddle and inclined to fix flawed measures while preserving voter intent.
These two literatures disagree, and the disagreement is unresolved.

Tracking. Ballotpedia maintains counts of which initiatives were altered and catalogues each state’s rules. Advocacy organizations track
the phenomenon episodically.

Method. Comparative politics has tools for tracking amendments to legislative text — minimum-edit-distance algorithms, and an
established literature measuring amendment success in EU legislation. These have never been applied to U.S. initiative alteration.


5.2 What does not exist
Any provision-level measurement of how much of a voter-enacted law survives. Existing counts are binary: altered or not. They cannot
distinguish a legislature implementing a measure from one hollowing it out. Ballotpedia acknowledges that alterations vary in how they
affect provisions and leaves the variation uncharacterized.


5.3 Why the gap exists, and the novelty claim
The gap is not an oversight. Measuring survival requires semantic judgment about legal effect, which was not tractable at scale until
recently.

Textual change and legal change come apart in both directions:

    A legislature can rewrite a provision entirely in new language and preserve its effect exactly. Edit distance scores this as a massive
    change. It is not.
    A legislature can leave a provision’s text untouched and add a nine-word exemption three sections away that guts it. Edit distance
    scores this as trivial. It is not.

The novelty claim, stated narrowly: established phenomenon, established method family, measurement nobody has taken. This is an
application novelty, not a contribution to natural language processing. It should be claimed as such and no further — overclaiming a
“novel AI technique” invites the question which one?, which has no good answer.


5.4 One additional finding the method may produce
Trackers observe whether a bill amended the initiative’s own statutory sections. That unit of observation is blind to at least four things:
collateral amendment of related statutes, definitional change elsewhere in the code, defunding through appropriations, and
recodification breaking the trail. The project’s exemption-sweep step is designed to catch the first two. Documented instances of
alteration that standard trackers missed would constitute a finding in their own right.




6. Research questions
Primary. What fraction of the provisions voters enacted survive legislative alteration intact, and what fraction are weakened or deleted?

Secondary. Does survival vary systematically by provision type? The working hypothesis is that legislatures preserve visible obligations
and strip the machinery — funding, indexing, enforcement, sunset.

Tertiary — doctrinal. Do state rule regimes affect survival? If rates do not differ between unrestricted states and purpose-fidelity
states, those constitutional protections are ceremonial.

Mechanism. By what drafting moves is hollowing-out accomplished? A typology of these moves is transferable well beyond ballot
initiatives.

Description. How are consequential amendments characterized by the legislatures that pass them?




7. Methodological precedent: the Frankenstein Variorum
The project’s structure is adapted from the Frankenstein Variorum Edition, a digital humanities project comparing five witnesses of Mary
Shelley’s novel — the manuscript, the 1818 edition, the Thomas copy, 1823, and 1831.


7.1 What transfers
The witness model. The Variorum treats each edition as a witness to one work. Here, the voter-enacted text, each amending bill, and
the current statute are witnesses to one law. This scales naturally to measures altered more than once.

The collation spine. Rather than tracking section numbers across versions, the Variorum maps every witness onto a version-
independent backbone, with each variation point carrying a stable ID. This is adopted directly, and it is the project’s answer to the
renumbering problem.

Heatmap as navigation. The Variorum’s landing page is an interactive heatmap — columns are witnesses, rows are locations in the
text, color is intensity of variation — explicitly designed to draw the reader in at moments of heavy alteration. This resolves the “what do
we highlight” problem: magnitude is rendered across the whole corpus and the eye finds the hot spots.

Method and Data as first-class pages. The Variorum publishes its pipeline and underlying data as navigational sections, not buried
appendices. This is the difference between a resource and a demonstration.

Dated snapshot. The Variorum’s generated pages state when they were produced. This project adopts the same convention (see §12.2).


7.2 What does not transfer, and what this project adds
The Variorum encodes magnitude only. Its heatmap indicates that a passage changed substantially; it does not indicate direction,
because for literary variants direction is not well-defined.

Legal variants have direction. A deletion narrows; an added exemption narrows; a raised threshold could go either way. This project’s
visualization therefore requires a second visual channel — intensity for magnitude, hue for direction.
This is the project’s contribution relative to its model, and it is a strong position to present from: here is the established digital-
humanities method; here is the dimension legal text requires that literary text does not.

A second divergence: the Variorum compares five witnesses of one work. This project compares roughly fifteen different laws with two or
three witnesses each, which requires a cross-case dimension the Variorum has no counterpart for.

A third: automated collation tools assume closely related texts. Statutory amendment restructures more aggressively than a novelist
revising, which is why the alignment step requires semantic matching rather than an off-the-shelf collation engine.




8. The spine data model

8.1 Definition
The spine is a data model, not a visualization. It stores the correspondence between versions by giving each provision an identity that
persists regardless of renumbering, relocation, or rewording.


8.2 Rules
The base witness is the voter-enacted text. Spine IDs are assigned there and never change.

Legislature-added provisions receive IDs, flagged as having no initiative counterpart. This distinction matters: added provisions
are frequently where exemptions live.

IDs are human-legible. Format: MO-2024-PropA-p017 — jurisdiction, year, measure, provision number. These will be read constantly
during hand-verification, and opaque hashes make that miserable.

Restructuring is one-to-many by design. When one provision splits into three, all three map to the same spine ID. The relationship
lives in the mapping, not in a label. This is why the schema has a single restructured tag rather than separate split and merged tags.


8.3 What depends on the spine
   Renumbering handling. Renumbering becomes a property visible in the data rather than a tag to maintain.
   Cross-case comparison. Claims like “funding provisions were stripped in nine of fifteen cases” require comparable provision
   identity across states.
   Citability. A stable ID lets a researcher cite a specific change in a footnote and have it resolve years later. Without it, every
   reference is “the third paragraph of the amended section, as of the 2026 snapshot.”


8.4 Record template

describes any real statute. A worked example drawn from actual coded output belongs in a later version of this document.

Notes on the template.

spine_id uses the two-letter state postal code, the year voters approved the measure, a short measure slug, and a zero-padded three-
digit provision number — for example the seventeenth provision of a 2024 Missouri measure called Proposition A. The number is
assigned in reading order on the base witness and never reassigned.

comparison_witness.text is null where the aligner returned none . Distinguishing a genuine absence from a retrieval failure is an open
question (§9 of the prototype specification) and will require an additional field once resolved.

One bill section may touch several spine IDs, and one spine ID may map to several sections after restructuring. The relationship is many-
to-many and lives in the witness mapping, not in these records.

Bill-level and measure-level blocks are denormalized into each provision record for readability during hand-verification. A production
store would normalize them; at this scale, the duplication costs nothing and makes a single record legible on its own.
9. The tagging schema

9.1 The layering principle
The schema separates what the text objectively did from what the change means. A tool that fuses these can be dismissed as
editorializing.

Layers 1, 2, 6, 7, 8 and 9 are near-objective. Layers 3 and 4 are interpretive. Layer 5 is mixed. Reliability is reported separately per layer,
so a skeptical reader can accept the structural coding while disputing the interpretive coding.

This separation is also what makes the tool usable by someone who disagrees with the project’s conclusions.


9.2 Layer 1 — Structural operation
Provision-level. What the text objectively did.

unchanged · relocated · rewritten · partially deleted · deleted entirely · added · restructured (split or merged) · replaced ·
suspended · repealed and reenacted · cross-reference altered · text unchanged but a relied-on term redefined elsewhere

Notes: repealed and reenacted is retained because it is a drafting device capable of laundering substantive change. The final value is
objectively verifiable but only detectable through the whole-statute sweep described in §10.4.

Deliberately excluded: reworded without substantive change (a Layer 3 judgment misfiled in an objective layer — code rewritten here
and clarifies in Layer 3) and renumbered only (made redundant by the spine).


9.3 Layer 2 — Provision type
Provision-level.

 scope/coverage (includes eligibility) · core obligation or entitlement · rate, amount, or threshold · escalation, indexing, or adjustment
· funding source · appropriation amount · enforcement authority · penalty or remedy · private right of action · administrative
procedure (includes notice, recordkeeping, appeals and review) · rulemaking authority and agency discretion · reporting and data
collection · exemption or carve-out · definitions · effective date · sunset or expiration · conflict or preemption clause

Two departures from the obvious list, both load-bearing:

Escalation is separate from core obligation. Missouri Proposition A is the argument: the $15 wage survived and the CPI indexing did
not. Folded together, the finding disappears.

Definitions are their own type. Redefining “employer” or “covered entity” can hollow out a statute without touching a single
obligation. It is the quietest mechanism available.

Deliberately excluded: findings and purpose statements (non-operative; used as input to purpose extraction, not tracked for survival) and
severability (boilerplate, near-universal, rarely altered meaningfully).


9.4 Layer 3 — Directional effect
Provision-level. Interpretive.

expands · narrows · delays · clarifies · restructures (no effect change) · administratively implements · indeterminate or contested

Explicit methodological commitment, to be stated on the Method page: direction is measured relative to the initiative’s own
stated purpose, not against any external baseline. This is contestable; stating it openly is what makes it defensible.

indeterminate or contested is the honesty valve. A direction scheme with no escape hatch forces false precision, and the rate at which
the valve is used is itself reportable.


9.5 Layer 4 — Mechanism
Provision-level. Applied only to provisions coded narrows or delays .

 outright repeal · definitional narrowing · exemption insertion · threshold adjustment · coverage carve-out · enforcement weakening
(removal or penalty reduction) · private right of action removal · discretion transfer (shall → may) · delegation without standards ·
effective-date delay · funding removal or redirection · sunset insertion · preemption of local implementation · procedural burden
insertion · burden of proof shift · reporting removal · conditioning on an external event · referendum-proof clause attachment

This layer is the project’s substantive contribution. A typology of how legislatures hollow out laws transfers well beyond ballot initiatives.

Two values deserve particular attention. enforcement weakening and discretion transfer both preserve the duty while removing what
makes it a duty, and both are invisible to any method measuring textual change.

This layer is kept deliberately generous. An unobserved mechanism is a cheap empty column; an unanticipated mechanism is a coding
crisis mid-project.


9.6 Layer 5 — Description and justification
Bill-level.
Stated rationale: technical correction · clarification · implementation · conflict resolution · fiscal concern · unintended
consequence · constitutional concern · compliance burden · federal conformity · none stated

Source of description: bill title · official summary · fiscal note · sponsor statement · governor's message

Floor statements and committee reports are excluded — retrieval cost is disproportionate and preservation is inconsistent across states.

Derived: description–effect gap (binary flag), set where the stated rationale is procedural and the measured effect is substantive.

This flag is the project’s headline generator. A bill described as a technical correction that deletes an enforcement provision is not a
percentage — it is a specific, citable instance. The gap is recorded as a binary rather than a magnitude score, because a magnitude
layered on two interpretive judgments compounds error in a way that is hard to defend.


9.7 Layer 6 — Temporal and procedural
Bill-level. Objective.

Temporal: days from voter enactment to bill introduction · days from voter enactment to alteration effective · altered before or after the
provision took effect · legislative sessions elapsed · alteration sequence number

Procedural: standalone or omnibus · session type (regular, special, lame-duck) · passage margin per chamber · supermajority required
and whether met · party control relative to initiative direction · signed, vetoed and overridden, or unsigned · emergency clause · adopt-
and-amend maneuver · committee of origin

The before/after-effect field matters: repealing something that has been operating for four months is a different political act than
repealing it before anyone felt it.

Known data issue: committee of origin availability varies sharply by state, and some legislatures do not record it machine-readably.
Blanks must distinguish “no committee” from “not retrievable.”


9.8 Layer 7 — Measure context
Measure-level.

 initiative route (direct/indirect) · vehicle (statute/constitutional) · vote margin · campaign funding profile · topic · state rule regime
· prior_similar_measure_year · subsequent_similar_measure_year

On the similar-measure fields: no time threshold is applied. The year of the nearest prior and subsequent similar measure is stored,
not a yes/no verdict within a window. This costs nothing extra at coding time and makes the window a query parameter rather than a
baked-in assumption, so a later user can ask five years, ten, or ever. The same principle governs the exclusion of election context ,
which is derivable from the year.

The search stopping rule is fixed: same policy domain, same state, state ballot measure history only.


9.9 Layer 8 — Subsequent fate
Measure-level. This is the layer that makes the resource continuing rather than static.

litigated and outcome · struck down in whole or part · re-initiated by campaign · constitutional amendment pursued afterward · altered
again in a later session · referendum filed to overturn the alteration · provision re-enacted in substantially the same form later


9.10 Layer 9 — Coding metadata
Not findings. Infrastructure — but a scholarly tool is not credible without it.

witness ID · source span offsets · coded by (model/hand/both) · model self-consistency across runs · human–model agreement · flagged
for review · adjudicated · confidence


9.11 What the schema deliberately omits
There is no severity or “gutted” field. That is the reader’s inference from Layers 1 through 4. Building it in converts the tool into an
argument the reader cannot audit.




10. Technical methodology

10.1 The claim about what this measures
Comparative politics measures textual change with minimum-edit-distance methods. This project measures whether the legal obligation
survived, which requires semantic judgment. That is the entire technical argument, and it should be stated in one sentence rather than
dressed up.


10.2 Embeddings
Anthropic does not serve an embeddings endpoint. Voyage AI publishes a legal-domain embedding model (the voyage-law- line; current
version to be checked at implementation time) trained on case law and statutory text. Generic embedding models handle legal prose
poorly because the domain vocabulary is dense with defined terms and cross-references.

Fallback: sentence-transformers with a BGE or MPNet model, run locally. Slower and less accurate on legal text, but free and
dependency-light.

No vector database. At a few thousand provisions, embeddings live in a NumPy array and similarity is a matrix multiplication. Pinecone
or Chroma would be overhead with no benefit.


10.3 Hybrid retrieval
Dense retrieval alone fails on legal text in a specific way: statutes carry distinctive lexical signals — defined terms, section numbers,
dollar amounts, cross-references — that embeddings smooth out. “Fifty breeding dogs” and “seventy breeding dogs” are near-identical in
embedding space, and that difference is precisely what the project exists to catch.

Two retrievers, fused:

   Dense. Embed each voter-enacted provision; retrieve top candidates from the amended statute by cosine similarity.
   Sparse. BM25 over the same corpus ( rank_bm25 ). Catches exact term and numeric matches.
   Fusion. Reciprocal rank fusion. A dozen lines of code, meaningfully better than either alone.

Output: five candidate counterparts per provision, passed to the classifier. Nothing is decided by similarity score.


10.4 Classification: three distinct calls
All use structured output schemas, returning validated JSON rather than parsed prose.

Call 1 — Alignment adjudication. Input: one original provision and its five candidates. Output: which candidate is the counterpart, or
none . The none branch is essential — forced matching is the primary failure mode, and provisions returning none become candidate
deletions for hand verification.

Call 2 — Effect classification. Input: the matched pair. Output: Layer 1 and Layer 3 values, plus a one-sentence rationale and the
specific span that drove the judgment. The rationale is not decoration; it is what gets read when auditing disagreements.

Call 3 — The exemption sweep. For each provision classified intact , a second retrieval pass over the entire amended statute searches
for limiting language — exemptions, carve-outs, thresholds, effective-date restrictions — and the model judges whether any of it
constrains the provision. Any provision failing this check is reclassified as narrows , with the Layer 4 mechanism recorded.

This step is the one nobody else does, and it is the project’s most defensible technical claim. Without it, Missouri Proposition A’s
minimum wage provision would score as fully intact, missing that the indexing mechanism was stripped.


10.5 Reliability machinery
   Self-consistency. Calls 2 and 3 run five times at low temperature; majority wins. The disagreement rate is a measured property of
   the instrument and is reported, not hidden.
   Cross-model check. Classification is re-run on a subsample with a second model family. Convergence is stronger evidence than one
   model’s output; divergence marks the hard cases for hand review.
   Position-bias control. Candidate order is randomized in Call 1. Language model judges favor earlier options.
   Contamination control. Measure names, numbers, and years are stripped before classification, so the model reasons from the text
   in front of it rather than from what it knows about Proposition A.


10.6 Pipeline hygiene
Every API call is cached, keyed on a hash of its input, stored as JSON on disk. The pipeline will be re-run many times as the codebook
evolves; without caching, each run re-pays and re-waits. Caching also makes the work reproducible for the archived version.

One record per provision: original text, span offsets, matched counterpart, all layer values, rationale, exemption-sweep result, and
agreement across runs. That table is the dataset.


10.7 Environment
Python, built through Claude Code. Session orchestration via Herdr, which allows long extraction runs to detach and survive a closed
laptop — orchestration, not compute.




11. Corpus and case selection

11.1 The tiered structure
The pipeline is run on all known altered initiatives (currently ~30 from Ballotpedia’s 2010–2024 window, extensible earlier and later).
Of these, fifteen are published as fully hand-verified case pages; the remainder are published as machine-coded and clearly marked
pending review.
This structure does three things at once. Aggregate patterns draw on the full set. Deep inspection draws on the verified fifteen. And the
verified/pending distinction makes the project continuable — the obvious next contribution is verifying more cases, which is the long-
term plan.


11.2 Why fifteen, and not thirty
A browsable tool rewards depth per case: every provision visible, every claim traceable to source text, enough hand-verification to
defend any individual cell. A wrong cell is visible on a case page in a way it is not in an aggregate table. Fifteen deeply verified cases is
more defensible than thirty shallow ones.

The number is provisional. The correct procedure is to hand-code one case, time it honestly, and multiply.


11.3 Selection design
The fifteen are selected as a diverse-case design — chosen to maximize variance on theoretically relevant dimensions, not to mirror a
population. Six dimensions, in rough order of importance:

1. Rule regime — unrestricted, restricted, purpose-fidelity. Must be covered.
2. Severity — total repeal, partial hollowing, technical correction, implementation. The full range, deliberately.
3. Topic — marijuana and elections (the two most-altered categories), plus labor, health, and at least one outlier.
4. Speed — South Dakota repealed in two months; Utah took two years.
5. Route — direct and indirect. Adopt-and-amend exists only on the indirect track.
6. Outcome — litigated and not.

Cases that cut against the hypothesis are mandatory. If all fifteen show hollowing-out, the site is an indictment wearing a tool’s
clothing. Genuine technical-correction cases, implementation cases where alteration furthered the purpose, and unaltered initiatives as
controls must be included.

11.5 The selection-bias defense
Three structural commitments, all of which must actually be honored:

1. The pipeline runs on all known cases, not only the published fifteen.
2. Selection criteria are published on the Method page before coding begins.
3. Excluded cases are listed with reasons.

The prepared answer to the objection:

     “These aren’t a sample — they’re selected to span the range. Every case in the corpus is machine-coded; fifteen are hand-verified
     and marked as such. The criteria are published, the excluded cases are listed, and the method is reproducible on any case you think
     I’ve missed.”

This sentence only holds if the three commitments above are true. If any slips, it becomes a claim that cannot be backed, which is worse
than not having it.
11.6 Case list verification
Ballotpedia’s count is a starting point, not the corpus. The first corpus task is verifying the case list against primary sources — checking
each measure’s statutory sections directly rather than trusting the amended/not-amended label. Some tracked alterations will prove
genuinely trivial on reading. Some untracked alterations may surface. The working number will shift, and both directions of shift are
reportable.




12. Validation

12.1 Per-layer reliability
Agreement is reported separately by layer, not in aggregate. Expected ceilings differ: Layer 1 near-perfect, Layer 2 high, Layers 3 and
4 moderate. An aggregate figure hides exactly the variation a reader needs.

Publishing a lower figure for the interpretive layers is not a weakness. It is the honest statement that direction coding is a judgment, and
it is what earns the right to publish the judgment at all.


12.2 The four checks
Independent hand-coding. A substantial sample coded by hand, blind to model output, with agreement reported. Ideally a second
human codes a subset, establishing the human-to-human ceiling — a model cannot meaningfully exceed the reliability of the task itself,
and knowing that ceiling tells you whether moderate agreement is mediocre or excellent.

Negative controls. Feed the aligner an unrelated statute; confirm it reports non-matches rather than forcing a pairing. This is a five-
minute test that catches the catastrophic failure mode.

Synthetic perturbation. Programmatically delete a provision known to survive; confirm the pipeline flags it as absent. Repeated across
provision types, this generates a large labeled test set without hand-coding any of it, and yields a detection rate broken out by provision
type. This is more methodologically serious than swapping in a larger model.

Unmatched-provision rate. Reported openly, since that is where errors concentrate.




13. The deliverable

13.1 Structure
A static site. Pipeline outputs JSON; a single page renders it. No backend. This matters for three reasons: it is publishable on Kenyon’s
digital archive, it is archivable, and it cannot rot the way a database-backed application does.

Case view — provision by provision, voter-enacted text beside current statute, coded by operation and direction, with the source span
for every claim, and the legislature’s own description of each change placed adjacent to its measured effect.

Cross-case views — a provision-type heatmap (types by cases, color by survival), a timeline (days from enactment to alteration), and a
description-gap view.

Method page — the pipeline, the codebook, the selection criteria, the excluded cases, per-layer reliability figures.

Data page — the full dataset, downloadable.

Detailed visual design is deferred and is not covered by this document. Two commitments are fixed: the design must let the eye find the
hot spots, and magnitude and direction must occupy separate visual channels.


13.2 Dated snapshot, not a living tracker
The site carries an explicit vintage. The repository contains the code that generated it. Documentation explains how to add a case.

This is deliberate. Undergraduate capstones reliably stop being updated the week after submission, and a dashboard claiming currency it
does not have is worse than an honest snapshot. A dated, reproducible snapshot is also more defensible in an interview than a “live
tracker” frozen at 2026.


13.3 The opening claim
The site must open with a claim, not a search box. A browsable interface with no thesis is a data visualization exercise, and the faculty
advisor has already pushed back once on whether this project would yield anything.

The claim is provisional until the data supports one. The schema was designed so that four candidate claims are available:

   From Layers 2 and 3 — legislatures preserve visible obligations and strip the mechanisms that sustain them.
   From Layer 4 — hollowing-out is accomplished through a small, recurring repertoire of drafting moves, and here is the typology.
   From Layer 5 — consequential amendments are routinely described in procedural language.
   From Layer 6 — alteration clusters in identifiable procedural windows.
Which one leads will be known by roughly the eighth or ninth coded case.




14. Limitations and scope conditions
To be stated on the Method page, not buried:

1. Initiated statutes only. Constitutional amendments are out of scope, which excludes several of the most-cited examples in this
   area.
2. Formal legal change only. The measure does not capture non-enforcement, underfunding through administrative discretion, or
   slow-walking — alternative routes to the same outcome. Gerber and colleagues found exactly this kind of implementation subversion,
   and no text-based method can register it.
3. Small n. Fifteen verified cases and roughly thirty machine-coded ones support description and typology, not hypothesis testing.
   Rule-regime comparisons are suggestive; Arizona correlates with everything else about Arizona.
4. Direction is a judgment. Measured against the initiative’s stated purpose. Contestable, and stated as such.
5. Coverage gaps. Some cases will fail text retrieval. The resolution rate is reported and the pattern of missingness described.
6. Ballotpedia-derived starting list. The corpus begins from a tracker whose unit of observation has known blind spots (§5.4).




15. Roadmap
Stage 1 — Prototype. Two cases, hand-coded and pipeline-coded, compared. Covered by the companion document.

Stage 2 — Case list verification. Audit the Ballotpedia-derived list against primary sources. Produce the confirmed corpus.

Stage 3 — Fifteen verified cases. Full schema, full validation, published.

Stage 4 — Full machine-coded corpus. All known cases, marked pending review.

Stage 5 — Site and paper. The paper is the argument; the site is the evidence. Whether the site or the paper is the primary deliverable
is a question for the faculty advisor, and it changes how the final three weeks are spent.

Beyond the semester. Hand-verify the remaining cases. Extend the window earlier than 2010 and later than 2024.

Deferred extensions, recorded so they are not lost:

   Justification analysis. How legislators publicly justify overriding a popular vote — floor statements, committee testimony, sponsor
   memos. Currently deferred on retrieval-cost grounds; a natural second half of the project.
   Vehicle choice, large-n. Code every initiative in the twenty-one states on one dimension: statutory or constitutional. Several
   hundred measures rather than thirty. The question: do campaigns increasingly choose the constitutional route, and does that shift
   happen faster in states where legislatures can alter statutes freely? This is the escalation cycle made measurable, and it would give
   the project a large-n behavioral layer beneath its small-n measurement layer.




16. Glossary
Adopt and amend — A legislature enacting an indirect initiative before the election to keep it off the ballot, then amending it under a
lower threshold.

Collation spine — A version-independent backbone onto which every version of a document maps; the mechanism for persistent
provision identity.

Diverse-case design — Case selection maximizing variance on theoretically relevant dimensions rather than mirroring a population.

Direct initiative — Qualifies for the ballot by signature alone.

Emergency clause — A legislative declaration that, in some states, blocks a veto referendum against the bill.

Exemption sweep — The pipeline step searching the whole amended statute for language limiting a provision that survived textually.

Indirect initiative — Goes to the legislature before reaching the ballot.

Initiated statute — A citizen-proposed ordinary law. The unit of analysis for this project.

Purpose-fidelity regime — A state rule permitting amendment only where it furthers the measure’s purpose.

Spine ID — A persistent, human-legible provision identifier, e.g. MO-2024-PropA-p017 .

Witness — One version of a legal text: the voter-enacted measure, an amending bill, or the current statute.
