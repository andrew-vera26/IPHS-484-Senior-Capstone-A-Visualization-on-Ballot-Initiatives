    Design references¶

    Researched November 15, 2026 from each site's own descriptive pages. The descriptions below come from page text, not from looking at the interfaces. Open each one yourself before relying on it. None of these is to be copied. The point is to borrow ideas.

    1. Constitute (constituteproject.org)¶

    Built by the Comparative Constitutions Project. Passages of the world's constitutions are tagged with 300+ topics. Users search by topic, filter by region and period, and read in a List view or a Compare view that puts two constitutions side by side. Excerpts can be pinned and exported to Google Docs, PDF or CSV. Every search, comparison and provision has a deep link. - Closest match to what we are building: tagged legal text, side by side. - Borrow: topic tags as a way in, deep links to a single provision, pinning and export. - Avoid: a search-first design. Ours should let the eye find hot spots before any search.

    2. V-Dem Graphing Tools (v-dem.net/graphingtools.html)¶

    Expert-coded democracy indicators, shown through many views: map, line graph, radar, scatter, heat map, regional and thematic comparison between two years. - Borrow: the idea of several views over one dataset, and a before-and-after comparison between two points in time. This maps onto comparing a law before and after an amending bill. - Avoid: a menu of a dozen chart types. We need one primary view done very well.

    3. Voteview (voteview.com)¶

    UCLA's tool for every congressional roll call vote in history, shown on a US map and on an ideological map of legislators. Search and download tools are included. - Borrow: one spatial picture that makes a whole dataset legible at a glance, with every point clickable down to the underlying record. - Avoid: abstract scaling that readers must trust. Our encodings should be readable without a methods paper.

    4. Comparative Agendas Project (comparativeagendas.net)¶

    Policy activity coded into 20 major topics and 200+ subtopics, with a Trends Tool for graphing coded series over time for one country or several. - Borrow: a coded dataset that non-specialists can explore, and an honest codebook linked from the tool. - Avoid: a codebook that readers must leave the tool to understand.

    5. Juxta Commons (digital humanities text collation)¶

    A tool for comparing versions of a text. It offers three views: a heat map that overlays the texts and colors differences, a side-by-side view with lines connecting points of difference, and a histogram showing which portions changed most. - Borrow: the heat map as the first view, with the histogram idea as an at-a-glance summary of where change concentrated. This is closest to the Frankenstein Variorum approach. - Caution: the page describes a 2013 poster abstract. Check juxtasoftware.org for whether the tool is still available.

    Bonus: Legislative Influence Detector (DSSG fellowship)¶

    Finds text reused between state bills and model legislation. Results appear side by side with matching passages highlighted. - Borrow: highlighted overlap between two legal texts. - Caution: the page is a project writeup from about 2015 to 2018, and I could not confirm the live tool still works.

    What these suggest for our site¶

    • Lead with an at-a-glance picture (heat map or strip), with detail one click away.
    • Make the side-by-side comparison of two legal texts the core reading view.
    • Give every provision a stable link.
    • Keep the codebook one click from every coded value.
    • Show how change concentrates, not only whether it occurred.
